<p class="lede">Good research starts with a decision, not a method. Before starting any research, ask: <strong>What do we need to learn right now to make the best decision?</strong></p>

That question guides everything else: the phase you're in, the method you'll use, and how you'll plan the work.

## From question to insight*

<p class="section-intro">Our planning process has six connected steps.</p>

<div class="research-steps">
<Steps>

1. ### Clarity

   Agree on the problem space and what decision needs to be made. Research is only useful when it connects to a real choice.

   **Start with what you already know.** Before planning new research, look for existing insights:

   - **Ask Dovetail** to search our research repository for past studies, findings, and recommendations. For example:
     - "What research have we done on document uploading?"
     - "What do we know about Medicaid enrollment challenges?"
     - "What have users said about [specific feature or service]?"
   - Ask stakeholders what research they've already done or commissioned.
   - Check analytics, support tickets, and other existing data sources.
   - Talk to team members who have direct contact with users.

   You might already have enough to move forward, or existing research might help you ask better questions. Then [match your work to the right phase](#match-your-work-to-the-right-phase).

2. ### Questions

   Turn your knowledge gaps into clear research questions. These aren't interview questions — they're what your research needs to answer.

   Be specific. Instead of "understand users," write: "What challenges do residents face when uploading documents for Unemployment Insurance?"

3. ### Refinement

   Decide what kind of answer you need and what evidence will be convincing, then choose the right scope and rigor. See [choose your approach](#choose-your-approach).

4. ### Methods

   Pick the method that best answers your question. The method isn't the goal — learning is.

5. ### Analysis and synthesis

   Plan how you'll make sense of results *before* you collect them. Decide:

   - How you'll capture and organize what you learn
   - What patterns or themes you're looking for
   - Who needs to be involved in analysis
   - How you'll move from observations to recommendations

   For synthesis and outputs, see [Share findings](../share-findings/).

6. ### Integration

   Think ahead: how will findings be used across the team and organization? Real value comes from:

   - Sharing insights in formats people can actually use (not just reports)
   - Connecting findings to decisions that need to be made
   - Building on previous research rather than starting over
   - Making research accessible in the repository for future teams

</Steps>
</div>

### Match your work to the right phase

<div class="glance-table">

| Phase | Where you are | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery | We don't know what we don't know yet. Learn about the problem space, users, and context. | Exploring how to make Unemployment Insurance more intuitive and equitable |
| Problem validation | Stakeholders think they know the problem. Confirm users experience it the same way. | Does document uploading actually frustrate users as much as we think? |
| Concept testing | Test whether ideas match user needs and expectations. | Would users welcome a digital intake form? |
| Solution testing | Confirm the solution is easy to use and works as expected. | Can users navigate a prototype intuitively? |
| Scaling and evaluation | After launch, uncover why unexpected behaviors or problems are happening. | Why did portal calls increase after our update? |

</div>

### Choose your approach

<div class="glance-table">

| Approach | What it gives you | Best when you need to |
|---|---|---|
| Desk research | What's already known, from reports, policies, analytics, and past research | Start any study. Always begin here. |
| Qualitative | The "why" behind behaviors, needs, and experiences | Understand motivations, uncover new problems, or explore how people make sense of something |
| Quantitative | The "what" and "how many" across larger groups | Prioritize issues, estimate demand, or prove something is happening at scale |
| Moderated | A facilitator who can probe and observe in real time | Dig deeper, test complex concepts, or support participants who need help |
| Unmoderated | Participants working on their own time | Get broad input quickly, see natural behavior, or test something straightforward |
| Field research | How systems work in people's real environment | See the actual context, not just what people describe |

</div>

Most research combines approaches. For example: start with desk research, conduct qualitative interviews to understand the problem, then use a quantitative survey to validate how widespread it is.

## Planning essentials

<p class="section-intro">Every research plan needs these.</p>

### Goals

What do we need to learn to make a decision? Share with peers in #ux-research or at Weekly Crit to ensure they're relevant and realistic.

### Recruitment

Represent New Jersey's diversity across geography, age, gender, culture, language, and abilities. Use UserInterviews.com for all participant management. See [Conduct research](../conduct/) for recruitment, consent, and data-handling guidance.

<Aside type="caution" title="Participant safety">
We do not research with participants currently in crisis or in crisis within the past 12 months.
</Aside>

### Screener

Every study needs a screener to make sure you're talking to the right people. A good screener:

- Confirms participants have relevant experience with the service, system, or problem
- Ensures demographic diversity across your sample
- Filters out professional research participants or those who don't meet your criteria
- Helps combat fraud (see below)

Write screening questions that are specific to your research, require knowledge only genuine participants would have, and can't easily be answered by AI or a quick web search.

<Aside type="tip" title="Example">
Instead of "Have you applied for unemployment benefits?" ask "What documents did you need to provide when you applied?" or "Describe a challenge you faced during the application process."
</Aside>

### Equipment

For in-person research, a wireless clip-on microphone system makes recordings much easier to transcribe in noisy places. Include equipment needs in your research plan, and book equipment early.

## Combating research fraud

Bots and scammers use AI to pass screeners, fake their way through moderated interviews, and dilute data through survey responses.

<Aside type="danger" title="Protect your research">
Use open-ended questions, attention checks, and real-time interactions to make research harder to fake.
</Aside>

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Screeners">
- Use open-ended questions that require specific, personal knowledge
- Ask for details that would be hard to fake (e.g., "What was confusing about the instructions on page 3?")
- Include attention-check questions buried in the screener
- Avoid questions that can be easily answered with a Google search
- Use CAPTCHA or verification steps in UserInterviews
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Moderated sessions">
- Turn on video and ask participants to keep cameras on
- Ask spontaneous follow-up questions that go off-script
- Ask participants to show you something physical (a document, their screen, their environment)
- Use activities that require real-time interaction (drawing, sorting cards, navigating a prototype)
- Pay attention to unnatural speech patterns, delays in response, or overly perfect answers
- Listen for generic responses that could apply to any situation
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Surveys">
- Include attention-check questions (e.g., "Please select 'strongly agree' for this question")
- Use open-ended questions that require context and specificity
- Check for duplicate IP addresses or suspicious patterns in response times
- Look for identical or near-identical responses across multiple participants
- Review responses before analysis
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Red flags">
- Vague, generic answers that lack personal detail
- Perfect grammar and overly formal language in casual conversations
- Delayed responses in video calls with audio that doesn't match lip movement
- Resistance to turning on video or showing identifying information
- Cookie-cutter responses that could have been copied and pasted
</TabItem>
</Tabs>

When in doubt, trust your instincts. If something feels off, it probably is. Document concerns and discuss with your team before marking a participant as completed.

## Good questions

<p class="section-intro">Keep questions short, clear, and neutral. Invite open, honest responses.</p>

| Instead of | Try |
|------------|-----|
| What do you like and dislike about the Anchor program? | Ask separately: What do you like? Then: What don't you like? |
| Do you prefer the option with the blue button? | Which option do you prefer and why? |
| Do you like these colors? | Why do these colors work (or not work) for you? |

## Using AI in research

<p class="section-intro">AI can be a valuable partner throughout the research process — but it works best when used thoughtfully and with clear boundaries.</p>

<Aside type="caution" title="Protect participant data">
Never enter personal or health information into an AI tool that isn't approved for that data. Use only the AI features built into tools your organization has approved, and de-identify data first whenever you can.
</Aside>

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Planning">
**NJ AI Assistant** — Structure research plans, draft interview guides, and refine research questions. AI can help you think through edge cases and suggest approaches you might not have considered.

**Ask Dovetail** — Search across our research repository to find related studies, identify what's already known, and discover knowledge gaps.

Use these tools early in planning, but always review suggestions critically and validate them with peers.
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="During sessions">
**AI transcription and note-taking** — When you're conducting research solo, AI transcription can capture what's being said so you can focus on the conversation.

- Always get explicit consent from participants before using AI transcription
- Explain what the tool does and how the data will be used
- Give participants the option to decline — and have a backup plan for manual notes
- Review transcripts for accuracy — AI makes mistakes, especially with accents, technical terms, or proper nouns
- Supplement transcripts with your own notes about body language, tone, and context
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Analysis">
**Ask Dovetail** — Identify patterns across qualitative data, generate initial themes, and organize findings. Especially useful with large volumes of interview notes or survey responses.

**NJ AI Assistant** — Structure findings and draft recommendations based on your analysis.
</TabItem>
</Tabs>

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="AI is a partner, not a replacement" icon="approve-check-circle">
    - Review AI-generated insights against your own observations
    - Validate patterns with your research team
    - Check for bias or misinterpretation
    - Ground recommendations in actual participant quotes and behaviors
    - Be transparent about where AI was used
  </Card>
  <Card title="What AI shouldn't do" icon="warning">
    - Make final decisions about research direction or recommendations
    - Replace human facilitators in research sessions
    - Interpret complex emotional or cultural nuances
    - Stand in for thorough, thoughtful analysis
    - Be used without participant knowledge or consent
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

AI is a tool that makes research more efficient. It doesn't make research better on its own — you do.
