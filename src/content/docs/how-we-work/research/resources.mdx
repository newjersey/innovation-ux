## Size the study first

<p class="section-intro">Not every study needs a long plan. Check again whenever the method, number of people, or session length changes.</p>

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Quick plan" icon="rocket">
    For small, remote studies with only a few sessions. Fits on one page: the decision, the research question, the method, and who to invite.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Full plan" icon="list-format">
    For in-person research, bigger budgets, or more sessions. Covers the four steps below, plus logistics, data handling, and budget.
  </Card>

## What a full research plan should cover

<div class="research-steps">
<Steps>

1. ### Clarity

   The decision the research will inform, who makes it, how big it is, and when it's due. Work your schedule back from that date.

2. ### What we know

   Past studies and findings on the topic. If it's been studied before, say why you still need the research.

3. ### Questions

   The research question, plus goals or hypotheses, each with what would prove it right.

4. ### Approach

   The lightest method that will answer the question, how many people, and a stop rule. For example, stop when two sessions in a row show nothing new. Check in halfway to decide whether to stop early or change course.

</Steps>
</div>

A full plan also covers:

- **Participants:** who you need, how you'll recruit them, and who you'll exclude, such as people currently in crisis. Write a screener that's hard to fake, and avoid collecting personal or health information you don't need.
- **Incentives:** one flat per-minute rate for every participant, based on scheduled time. Don't use separate rates for residents and professionals. If the flat rate can't reach a specific group, document why before you offer more. Pay participants the same day if you can, and within 2 business days at most. If a session can't happen because of a problem on your side, pay in full.
- **Consent and data handling:** your consent form, where recordings are stored, who can access them, and when they'll be deleted.
- **Timeline:** recruiting, a pilot session, sessions, de-identification, synthesis, and sharing. Try to fit it all in a two-week sprint.
- **Budget:** incentives plus any travel, meals, or supplies.
- **Risks and considerations:** sensitive topics, privacy, accessibility, digital access, language, and representation.
- **Approvals:** peer review for every study, plus whoever approves budget and scope in your organization. Don't contact participants until approvals are in.
- **Payment ready:** before the first session, confirm that funding is approved, you can access the payment account, the payment message is set up, and you've chosen how participants will be paid.

## How to structure a conversation guide

For most 1:1 interviews, 45 minutes is enough time for rapport, a real walkthrough, and a short prototype check, while keeping participants fresh and focused.

| Section | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Welcome and setup | 5 min | Introductions, purpose, consent to record, and a reminder not to share personal information |
| Background and context | 10 min | How they use the service today: why, how often, and what frustrates them |
| Deep dive | 15 min | Walking through a recent experience: pain points, workarounds, and expectations |
| Prototype or concept feedback | 10 min | First impressions and a think-aloud task, using made-up data only |
| Wrap-up | 5 min | Final thoughts, what happens next, and how they'll be paid |

Use 30 minutes for a single focused task. Save 60 minutes for co-design, complex services, or sessions with an interpreter.

**Plan ahead for sensitive moments.** Decide what you'll say if someone shares personal information, shows a real record on screen, asks for help with their own case, or gets upset. Offer a break, a skip, or a chance to stop, and pay them either way.
